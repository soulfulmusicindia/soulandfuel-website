If the end nearest the edge of the frame has grown an extra seat, the room was shot too wide.

Very wide lenses are everywhere in property photography because they solve a problem fast. Small room, nowhere to stand back. One frame swallows the whole thing.

What they take in return is proportion. Furniture near the edges stretches. A square living room comes out looking like a corridor with a sofa parked in it.

Designers and architects spend weeks getting proportion right. It is the first thing a client feels walking in, and the first thing a very wide lens gives away.

There is a cost on the booking side too. A hotel room shot at the widest setting looks generous on the website. The room is the same size when the guest opens the door. Their expectation is not, and that ends up in a review.

I would rather show two thirds of a room properly than all of it stretched. The part you can see is right, so the rest of the room reads right as well.

For a developer, the very wide photo wins the click and loses the site visit. For an architect, it flattens the ceiling height you argued three meetings for.

So when the photos of your next project come back, look at the corners and look at the sofa. That tells you more than scrolling the whole gallery.

Designers, has a set of photos ever come back looking bigger and less like the room you designed?
