## Topic bucket

Advice to clients, with a bit of good practice. How the sun decides the running order of a shoot day, and what that means when you book one.

## Hook

Most houses have about four good hours in them.

## Post

Most houses have about four good hours in them.

Not four hours of work. Four hours where the light is in the room you happen to be standing in.

Before a shoot I ask for the floor plan, mostly to see which way the rooms face. That decides the running order of the whole day.

An east facing bedroom is finished by ten. After that the light goes flat and the windows blow out. The living room on the west side does nothing until about four, then gives you an hour worth waiting for.

North facing rooms are the generous ones. Soft light most of the day, no hurry.

I shoot with every light in the building switched off. Lamps, cove lighting, downlights, all of it. So daylight is the only thing I have, and daylight arrives one room at a time.

Clients sometimes ask why we cannot start with the best room. Usually because the best room is a five o'clock room and it is nine in the morning.

For a hotel this is the difference between one day and two. Rooms facing four directions will not fit into a single afternoon, whatever the day rate suggests.

For a house it saves an argument later. If the room you are proudest of faces north, we can shoot it any time and spend the good hours elsewhere.

So when you book, send the floor plan with the orientation marked. It saves more time than anything else on the list.

Designers, do you already know which of your rooms is the five o'clock room?
